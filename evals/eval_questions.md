# PCOS RAG Eval Questions


## PCOS Core


**Q1: What are the Rotterdam criteria for diagnosing PCOS?**
Answer: For diagnosing PCOS, the Rotterdam criteria are : 
For adults, it requires presence of two of the following: i) clinical or bio chemical hyperandrogenism. ii) ovulatory dysfunction and iii) polycystic ovaries on ultrasound or elevated anti-mullerian hormone (AMH) levels, after other causes of these features are excluded. Where irregular menstrual cycles and hyperandrogenism are present, ultrasound or AMH are not required for diagnosis. 
In adolescents, both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required, and ultrasound and AMH is not recommended, because of its poor specificity.
Source: ESHRE 2023, Abstract, Page [5]


**Q2: How many of the Rotterdam criteria need to be met for a PCOS diagnosis?**
Answer: Two (2) of the Rotterdam criteria need to be met for a PCOS diagnosis for adults. Also for adults, Where irregular menstrual cycles and hyperandrogenism are present, ultrasound or AMH are not required for diagnosis, whereas in the case of adolescents, both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required, with ultrasound and AMH not recommended. 
Source: ESHRE 2023, Abstract, Page [5]


**Q3: What is the difference between PCOS subtypes?**
Answer: The document discusses the condition's clinical heterogeneity and variation in presentation. While different phenotypes exist (e.g., based on combinations of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and PCOM), the guideline does not provide a specific breakdown of subtypes.
Source: Page [8] (context and background)


**Q4: What hormones are typically elevated in PCOS?**
Answer: Testosterone, free testosterone (estimated by the free androgen index), androstenedione, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS). Luteinizing hormone (LH) is also affected, and increased LH secretion or elevated LH/FSH ratio in PMOS/PCOS may influence fertility.
Source: Page[161]

**Q6: What is polycystic ovarian morphology and how is it detected?**
Answer: Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM), in the Rotterdam criteria was defined as 12 or more follicles measuring 2-9mm throughout the entire ovary or an ovarian volume greater or equal to 10ml.
Page[65]
Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) is a diagnostic feature characterized by follicle excess and/or ovarian enlargement. It is detected via ultrasound, with thresholds for adults being a follicle number per ovary (FNPO) of ≥ 20 or an ovarian volume (OV) of ≥ 10 ml. It can also be defined using serum AMH levels as an alternative to ultrasound in adults.
Source: Recommendations 1.4.3, 1.4.4, and 1.5.1, Pages[24, 25]


**Q7: Is PCOS only a reproductive condition?**
Answer: No. The renaming to Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) emphasizes that it is a multisystem condition involving endocrine, metabolic, reproductive, dermatological, and psychological features, with increased risks for cardiovascular disease and diabetes.
Source: Page 2 (Why the name needed to change), Page 3 (What's in the new name: PMOS)


**Q8: At what age is PCOS typically diagnosed?**
Answer: It can be diagnosed in adolescents (defined as 10-19 years by WHO) and reproductive-aged women. The guideline emphasizes that the condition is considered enduring/lifelong.
Source: Page 11 (Population), Recommendation 1.7.1, Page 32


**Q9: Can you have PCOS without irregular periods?**
Answer: Yes, but the criteria still require two out of three features. You could have hyperandrogenism and PCOM without regular cycles. The guideline also notes that ovulatory dysfunction can occur even with regular cycles.
Source: Recommendation 1.1.5, Page 21


**Q10: What is the prevalence of PCOS globally?**
Answer: The prevalence is estimated at 10-13% globally using the Rotterdam criteria. It is similar across world regions and ethnicities but may be higher in South East Asian and Eastern Mediterranean regions.
Source: Recommendations 1.6.1 and 1.6.2, Page 26


**Q11: How is PCOS diagnosed in adolescents differently than adults?**
Answer: In adolescents, both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required. Ultrasound and AMH are not recommended for diagnosis due to poor specificity and overlap with normal pubertal physiology.
Source: Page 5 (Abstract), Recommendations 1.4.6 and 1.5.4, Pages 25-26


**Q12: What is the role of anti-Müllerian hormone in PCOS diagnosis?**
Answer: Serum anti-mullerian hormone is used to diagnose PCOM in adults as an alternative to ultrasound, but it should not be used as a single diagnostic test. It is not recommended to use in adolescents.  
Source: Page 25 Recommendations 1.5.1, 1.5.2, 1.5.3, Page 26 recommendations 1.5.4.


**Q13: Can PCOS go away on its own?**
Answer: The guideline states that a diagnosis of PMOS/PCOS is considered enduring/lifelong. However, symptoms can evolve (e.g., menstrual cycles may become more regular with age).
Source: Recommendation 1.7.1, Page 26; Section 1.7 Justification, Page 66


**Q14: What lifestyle changes are recommended for PCOS management?**
Answer: Healthy lifestyle behaviours encompassing healthy eating and physical activity are recommended to optimize general health, body composition, and weight management. There is no single best diet or exercise plan, but activity should align with general population guidelines.
Source: Recommendations 3.1.2, 3.3.2, 3.4.2, Pages 39-40, Summary of narrative review evidence, Page 98.
 
**Q15: Is there a cure for PCOS?**
Answer: No. The document describes PMOS/PCOS as a lifelong condition, and there is no mention of a "cure." Management focuses on preventing complications, managing symptoms, and treating associated health risks.
Source: Recommendation 1.7.1, Page 32


**Q16: What is the relationship between PCOS and insulin resistance?**
Answer: Insulin resistance is a core pathophysiological feature. It is present in 75% of lean women and 95% of women with a higher BMI and is a key driver of the condition's clinical features.
Source: (What's in the new name: PMOS); Clinical need for question 4.3, Page 117


**Q17: What percentage of people with PCOS have insulin resistance?**
Answer: The guideline states that hyperinsulinaemia is present in 85% of those affected.
Source: Page 3 (What's in the new name: PMOS)


**18: How does insulin resistance worsen PCOS symptoms?**
Answer: The document notes that insulin resistance is an underlying endocrine/hormonal feature and contributes to the metabolic aspects of the condition. It is further exacerbated by excess weight.
Source: Section 3.1 Clinical need, Page 102


**19: What is metformin and how is it used in PCOS?**
Answer: Metformin is an insulin sensitizer. It is recommended in adults with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² for improving metabolic outcomes (insulin resistance, glucose, and lipid profiles). It is not the first-line treatment for all PCOS features, and its use is primarily for metabolic indications.
Source: Recommendation 4.3.1, Page 38; Recommendation 4.4.2, Page 39, 4.3 Metformin, clinical need,  page 117


**20: Does everyone with PCOS need metformin?**
Answer: No, metformin is not for everyone with PCOS. Its use depends on the clinical context. In non-pregnant individuals, it is specifically recommended for adults with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² to improve metabolic outcomes (insulin resistance, glucose, and lipid profiles), with limited evidence for use in those with a lower BMI. In pregnancy, metformin is not routinely recommended, as it has not been shown to prevent gestational diabetes, miscarriage, hypertension, or pre-eclampsia; however, it may be considered in specific circumstances, such as when there is a risk of preterm birth, to reduce preterm delivery and limit excess gestational weight gain, though long-term offspring effects remain unclear.
Source : Recommendations 4.3.1, 4.3.3 Page 38, 4.11.1, , 4.11.2, , 4.11.3 Page 43.


**21: What dietary approaches are recommended for PCOS and insulin resistance?**
Answer: The guidelines suggest that a healthy lifestyle is very important, but that no one specific diet or exercise plan is better than any other and patient preferences are most important here. The focus is on sustainable, healthy eating tailored to individual preferences, consistent with general population guidelines for healthy eating.
Source: Plain Language Statement Page 7, Recommendations 3.3.1, 3.3.2 Page 34


**22: What is the risk of Type 2 diabetes in people with PCOS?**
Answer : In Type 2 Diabetes, both micro and macrovascular risks are increased. Regardless of age and BMI, women with PCOS have an increased risk of impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose tolerance, and type 2 diabetes. The risk is independent of, yet exacerbated by, BMI.
Source: 1.9 clinical need for questions, Page 69. Recommendation 1.9.1, Page 70. 


**23: How does weight affect PCOS symptoms?**
Answer: There are high rates of weight gain in people with PMOS/PCOS. Excess weight increases the clinical features of PCOS (hyperandrogenism, metabolic issues) and is a significant concern. Weight management is critical, but the guideline cautions against weight stigma.
Source: Section 3.1 Justification, Page 104; Recommendation 3.5.2 PP, Page 42. Clinical need, Page 99. 


**24: Can you have PCOS and be a healthy weight?**
Answer: Yes. The condition occurs across the BMI spectrum. The recommendations apply to women "from lean to higher weight."
Source: Page 11 (Population)


**25: What blood tests are used to assess metabolic health in PCOS?**
Answer: A lipid profile (cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides) is recommended at diagnosis. Blood pressure should be measured annually. Glycaemic status should be assessed at diagnosis and reassessed every 1-3 years, with the Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) being the most accurate test.
Source: Recommendations 1.8.3, 1.8.4, Page 68; Recommendations 1.9.2, 1.9.3, 1.9.9, Pages 70-71


**26: What is the connection between PCOS and cardiovascular health?**
Answer: Women with PCOS have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and potentially cardiovascular mortality, which is a new and strengthened recommendation in the guideline.
Source: Recommendation 1.8.1, Page 68; Summary of key changes, Page 50;
Clinical need for question, Page 67.


**27: How does exercise affect insulin resistance in PCOS?**
Answer: Exercise improves insulin resistance and offers a potentially effective approach to treating PMOS/PCOS, 
Source: 3.4 Exercise interventions, clinical need, Page 103.


**28: What is the role of inositol in PCOS management?**
Answer:  Inositol could be considered based on individual preferences, noting limited harm and potential for metabolic improvement, but limited clinical benefits (e.g., for ovulation or weight). Metformin is recommended over inositol for hirsutism and central adiposity. It is considered experimental for infertility.
Source: Recommendations 4.7.1, 4.7.2, and 5.8.1, Pages 40, 49


**29: How does PCOS affect cholesterol levels?**
Answer: Lifestyle interventions in PCOS are effective for improving lipid profiles. Metformin is also recommended for improving lipid profiles. The guideline recommends screening all women with PCOS for dyslipidaemia.
Source: Recommendations 3.1.1 and 4.3.1, Pages 33, 38; Recommendation 1.8.3, Page 27

**Q31: What is the prevalence of anxiety in people with PCOS?**
Answer: The prevalence is high, with moderate to severe anxiety symptoms being common. Reported prevalence of anxiety was up to 76.7% in the studies included in the systematic review.
Source: 2.2 Depression and anxiety, clinical need for question, Page 81; Summary of Systematic evidence, Page 81.


**Q32: Why does PCOS increase the risk of depression?**
Answer: The document confirms the high prevalence but does not provide a specific causal explanation. It notes that psychological symptoms may be related to distress associated with the condition's features.
Source: Recommendation 2.2.1, Page 30; Section 2.2 Justification, Page 82


**Q33: How does PCOS affect body image and self-esteem?**
Answer: Features of PCOS, such as hirsutism, acne, and weight gain, can have a negative impact on body image.
Source: Recommendation 2.4.1, Page 30; Section 2.4 Justification, Page 84


**Q34: What is the relationship between PCOS and eating disorders?**
Answer: Women with PMOS/PCOS have a higher prevalence of experiencing many of the identified risk factors for eating disorders. Eating disorders and disordered eating should be considered in PCOS, regardless of weight, especially in the context of weight management and lifestyle interventions.
Source: Recommendation 2.5.1, Page 85; Section 2.5 Justification, Page 85;


**Q35: Are there specific mental health screening recommendations for PCOS?**
Answer: Yes. Healthcare professionals should screen for depression in all adults and adolescents and for anxiety in all adults with PCOS, using regionally validated screening tools. Screening should be at diagnosis, with repeat screening based on clinical judgment, risk factors, comorbidities, and life events.
Source: Recommendations 2.2.1 and 2.2.2, Page 82; Recommendation 2.2.4 PP, Page 82


**Q36: How do PCOS symptoms like hair loss and acne affect mental health?**
Answer: The guideline notes the potential negative psychosocial impact of clinical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, hair loss, acne) and considers the reporting of these symptoms important regardless of severity. It also states that laser hair removal can improve depression, anxiety, and quality of life.
Source: Recommendation 1.3.4, Pages 23; Recommendation 2.8.2, Page 94; Recommendation 4.8.1, Page 41.


**Q37: What is the relationship between PCOS and sleep quality?**
Answer: Women with PCOS have a significantly higher prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) compared to those without PCOS, independent of BMI. Assessment for symptoms is recommended.
Source: Recommendations 1.10.1 and 1.10.2, Page 28


**Q38: Does treating PCOS symptoms improve mental health outcomes?**
Answer: Yes. The guideline states that lifestyle interventions, laser hair removal, and other therapies that target PCOS features have the potential to improve psychological symptoms.
Source: Recommendation 2.8.2 PP, Page 32


**Q39: What support resources exist for people with PCOS?**
Answer: The guideline mentions resources like the AskPMOS app and the Primary Care Tool for clinicians. It also recommends tailored, culturally appropriate, and inclusive information and resources, and encourages healthcare professionals to guide patients to evidence-based resources to avoid misinformation.
Source: Plain Language Statement, Page 7; Recommendations 2.6.1.1, 2.6.1.5, Page 31


**Q40: How does the diagnostic delay in PCOS affect mental health?**
Answer: The document states that women have reported a delayed diagnosis, poor diagnosis experience, and dissatisfaction with care. It also notes that the original name of the condition contributed to stigma and distress. The guideline emphasizes that sharing news on diagnosis should be done in an empathic and evidence-based manner to mitigate negative impacts.
Source: Page - (Why the name needed to change), Page 90 (Section 2.6.4 evidence summary), Recommendation 2.6.4.2, Page 32


**Q41: What specific medication dosage should I take for my PCOS?**
Answer: There is no specific dosage given in the guideline, it does not prescribe specific dosages for individual patients. It provides general dosage information, such as the suggested maximum daily dose of metformin (2.5 g in adults) and spironolactone doses (25-100 mg/day), but emphasizes that clinical judgment and individual patient characteristics must be used to determine dosages. It states this is not a prescribing guide.
Source: Recommendation 4.3.4 PP, Page 38; Recommendation 4.6.5 PP, Page 40; Page 14 (What the guideline does not address)

It's always recommended to go to a doctor to get your diagnosis checked, using their clinical judgment, knowledge, and expertise, considering the condition, circumstances, and perspectives of the individual affected, applying shared decision making in consultation with that patient and/or the parent/s or guardian/s.

**Q43: Which birth control pill is best for my PCOS?**
Answer: The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) could be recommended in reproductive age adults with PMOS/PCOS for management of hirsutism and/or irregular menstrual cycles. The guideline states that no specific COCP preparation can be recommended as being superior for PCOS. Healthcare professionals should consider general population guidelines, the lowest effective estrogen dose, and individual risk factors like weight. It specifically notes that 35 μg ethinyl estradiol plus cyproterone acetate should be considered second-line therapy.
Source: Recommendations 4.2.4 and 4.2.5, Page 37; Recommendation 4.2.1, Page 37.


**Q44: Can I get pregnant naturally with PCOS?**
Answer: Yes, those with PCOS/PMOS be reassured that "pregnancy can often be successfully achieved either naturally or with assistance."
Source: General principle, Page 43.


**Q45: Should I take inositol or metformin?**
Answer: The guideline recommends metformin over inositol for treating hirsutism and central adiposity. Inositol can be considered based on individual preferences and values, given limited harm but limited clinical benefits.
Source: Recommendations 4.7.1 and 4.7.2, Page 40.

**Q47: What is the best diet for PCOS weight loss?**
Answer: There's no single "best" diet for PCOS weight loss. Research found that low-carb, high-protein, DASH, and other diet types all work about the same for weight and metabolic health, as long as calories are reduced. Rather than chasing a specific diet plan, it's more effective to focus on healthy, balanced eating that includes fibre-rich carbs (like wholegrains, legumes, and fruit), is sustainable for you personally, and fits your food preferences and lifestyle since sticking with it long-term matters more than the specific diet type chosen. (pg: 100-101)
**Q48: How do I know if I have lean PCOS?**
Answer: You'd know you have lean PCOS if you meet the standard PCOS diagnostic criteria while being at a normal weight (BMI under 25). There's no separate test for it. Specifically, you need at least 2 of these 3: irregular or absent periods, signs of excess androgens (excess hair growth, acne, or elevated testosterone on bloodwork), and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. It's the same diagnosis as regular PCOS, just occurring in someone who isn't overweight. A doctor would confirm this with an exam, bloodwork, and possibly an ultrasound, rather than symptoms alone.(pg: 96-97)
**Q49: Will my PCOS symptoms get worse as I age?**
Answer: As you get older with PCOS, some symptoms can actually improve while others tend to stick around. Your periods often become more regular over time, and ovarian volume naturally decreases with age, similar to what happens in women without PCOS. However, PCOS itself doesn't go away; it's considered a lifelong condition, and androgen-related symptoms like excess hair growth (hirsutism) tend to persist even after menopause. Women with PCOS may also reach menopause a bit later than average, by about one and a half to two years. Overall, the guideline is honest that long-term research on PCOS as women age is still limited, so this isn't a fixed prediction; it's worth discussing your own situation with your doctor.(pg : 65-66)


**Q50: What supplements should I take for PCOS?**
Answer : Inositol – can be considered for PCOS, based on personal preference. It may help metabolic markers (like insulin resistance) with low risk of harm, but evidence for benefits on ovulation, hirsutism, or weight is limited and of very low certainty. No specific dose/form can be recommended yet due to lack of quality evidence.( pg :127- 129) Folate/ prenatal vitamins -  recommended specifically when trying to conceive (higher dose if BMI >30), not for general PCOS management.( pg :140- 142)
